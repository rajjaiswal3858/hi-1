Your Brand Now Has a Front Desk Inside ChatGPT

  • Andy Smith
  • November 17, 2026
  • Updated: November 17, 2026

On November 10, OpenAI started letting companies claim their identity inside ChatGPT and put their own agent behind it. The beta, called Businesses in ChatGPT, has two parts. Business Hub is where a company verifies its profile, corrects its details, uploads product feeds, and sees how often ChatGPT mentions it. Business Agents are branded assistants that eligible U.S. businesses can publish so people can ask them questions without leaving ChatGPT.

In our article on OpenAI's dots, we looked at customers sending their own agents to act for them. This launch is the other side of that. Now your business can have an agent waiting inside the assistant your customers already use, and OpenAI says 1.2 billion people use ChatGPT every week.

Most companies will treat this as a marketing checkbox: claim the listing, fill in the hours, move on. That undersells it. A Business Agent is a customer-facing system that speaks for you, on a platform you don't control, using whatever information you've given it. Building one is easy. Getting the information, rules, and handoffs behind it right is the work.

OpenAI's pages don't mention a fee for claiming a profile or publishing an agent. OpenAI says it plans to expand to more countries and business types, and eventually to offer tools for organizations with more complex needs.

The agent is only as good as what's behind it

OpenAI says a Business Agent answers using "the business's information." For most companies, that information isn't ready to answer customers on its own. It lives across a product catalog, a pricing system, a returns policy written for lawyers, a support knowledge base that was last cleaned up two reorganizations ago, and the heads of a few people on the support team.

A human rep fills those gaps without anyone noticing. An agent fills them with confident guesses. On your own website you can see that happen in the logs and fix it. Inside ChatGPT, you can't read the conversations. You'll mostly find out from customers who quote the agent back to you.

Three things decide whether this goes well.

Source of truth. Choose which systems feed the profile and the agent, and make sure what's published stays in sync with prices, stock, and policies that change daily. A product feed that's six hours stale will promise inventory you don't have.

Scope. Write down what the agent may answer, what it must decline, and where it should send people. Pricing exceptions, warranty disputes, account-specific questions, and anything regulated in your industry belong on the "send elsewhere" list until you've tested them.

Handoff. Decide where a conversation goes when the agent reaches its limit: your site, a phone line, a support queue. Make sure the receiving team knows it's coming. A dead-end chat in ChatGPT reflects on your brand, not on OpenAI's.

Read the dashboard with care

Business Hub gives marketing teams something they've wanted since AI search took off: a count of how often ChatGPT mentions them. That's useful. It's also easy to over-read.

OpenAI's own tooltip says clicks "don't confirm website visits or conversions" (Search Engine Roundtable). Early numbers vary widely. OpenAI's sample dashboard shows website clicks at roughly 35% of mentions. One of the first live dashboards PPC Land looked at showed 24 clicks on 690 mentions over 28 days, about 3.5%. OpenAI hasn't said what counts as a mention, what the reporting thresholds are, or whether claiming a profile affects how often you're recommended.

Treat these as directional signals and connect them to data you control: referral traffic in your own analytics, conversions tagged by source, and support volume. If the agent is working, you should see fewer basic questions reaching your team, not just a higher mention count.

The bigger issue is dependence. The profile, the agent, the analytics, and now the ad unit that points at the agent all live on one company's platform. OpenAI decides the rules for eligibility, verification, ranking, and reporting, and those rules will change during the beta. That isn't a reason to sit out. People are already asking ChatGPT about you, and an unclaimed profile means they get answers you had no part in. It is a reason to keep your product data, policies, and customer relationship in systems you own, with ChatGPT as one channel among several.

What to do before the beta widens

  • Claim and lock down the profile now. Even if you aren't eligible for an agent yet, verify ownership and correct your details. Decide who in the company owns the account and who approves changes. Early users have raised concerns about the claim process being used to hijack listings, so treat access like any other brand account.
  • Ask ChatGPT about yourself. Run the questions your customers ask: product comparisons, pricing, return policies, "is this right for me." Write down where the answers are wrong or out of date. That list is your first content backlog, and it tells you which sources need fixing before an agent repeats them.
  • Fix the source before you build the agent. Pick the systems that feed your profile and agent, make sure they stay in sync, and write the scope and handoff rules described above. If those rules don't exist for your own website chat, write them once for both.
  • Pilot narrowly and measure against your own data. If you're eligible, launch an agent on one product line or a small set of question types. Judge it by referral traffic, conversions, and support deflection in your own systems, not only by OpenAI's mention counts.
  • Decide your position on paid placement. Ads can now send people straight to your agent. Choose deliberately whether that belongs in your acquisition mix, before someone turns it on as an experiment.

The companies that do well here won't be the ones that claimed their profile first. They'll be the ones whose agent gives the same correct answer a good salesperson would, and hands off cleanly when it can't.
