01 · Definition

What is enterprise AI transformation, and how is it different from digital transformation?

Short answer

Enterprise AI transformation is the redesign of how a company decides, builds, and operates so that AI systems do real work inside core processes, with measurable P&L impact. Digital transformation moved processes onto software. AI transformation changes who, or what, executes them, and it only counts when it reaches production and people use it.

The distinction matters because the failure modes are different. Digital transformation failed when systems did not integrate. AI transformation fails when a model works in a demo and nobody redesigns the workflow around it. BCG's 2025 research found that organizations capturing value at scale follow a 10-20-70 rule: roughly 10% of the effort is algorithms, 20% is technology and data, and 70% is people and process. McKinsey's 2026 State of AI found that 73% of high performers redesigned workflows, versus 25% of everyone else.

At HatchWorks AI we frame the work in three stages that map to the three places programs break: Decide (which initiatives to fund, in what order), Build (production-grade systems, not prototypes), and Adopt (governance, enablement, and measurement so the capability sticks). A transformation is the sum of all three. A pilot is usually only the middle one.

Decide

Opportunity assessment, readiness scoring, prioritized roadmap by value, feasibility, and readiness. Typically 4 to 8 weeks.

Build

Intelligence layer, agents, data foundation, and AI-native products delivered through an AI-native operating model with quality gates.

Adopt

Adoption baseline, governance model, role-based enablement, and ongoing adoption reporting so usage and value are measured, not assumed.

02 · The pilot trap

Why do most enterprise AI pilots never produce measurable business value?

Short answer

Pilots stall for organizational reasons, not model reasons. The most common causes are picking use cases by novelty instead of value, no owner with budget authority, data that is not production grade, no path from prototype to a governed system, and no adoption plan. The MIT NANDA report found about 95% of organizations saw no measurable P&L impact from generative AI, and that partner-built solutions reached deployment about twice as often as internal builds.

The MIT NANDA report "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" studied 300 public deployments, surveyed 350 employees, and interviewed 150 leaders. Its headline finding is about value, not deployment: roughly 95% of organizations were getting no measurable P&L return from generative AI. Separately, it found strategic partnerships reached deployment 66% of the time versus 33% for internal development. S&P Global's 2025 survey found 42% of companies had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% a year earlier, and 46% of proofs of concept were scrapped before production. Gartner's 2026 survey of 1,303 organizations with $50M+ revenue found only 22% have scaled AI across multiple business units.

The five failure patterns we see most often

  1. Fifty ideas, no roadmap. Every function has a wishlist and nobody has scored them against value, feasibility, and data readiness. One general contractor came to us with 50+ AI ideas; the GenROI engagement narrowed them to four funded priorities and a 2026 roadmap with five workstreams.
  2. Demo data, production expectations. The prototype ran on a clean extract. Production has schema drift, duplicate records, and three ERPs.
  3. No accountable business owner. IT sponsors it, the business tolerates it, and nobody is on the hook for the metric.
  4. Developer-only tooling. Engineers got Copilot licenses. The rest of the organization got an email. Deloitte found 84% of companies have not redesigned jobs around AI.
  5. Pilot theater. The pilot was designed to be shown, not to be scaled. Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by end of 2027 for exactly this reason.

07 · Sourcing strategy

Should we build AI in-house, buy software, or bring in a partner?

Short answer

Buy where the problem is generic and the vendor's roadmap is yours too. Build where the capability is proprietary, touches your data moat, or becomes an asset buyers will value. Partner when you need to build but lack the production AI muscle today, and insist on IP ownership and knowledge transfer so the partner works themselves out of the job.

The data cuts against pure in-house builds: MIT NANDA found partnered solutions reached deployment twice as often, and Menlo Ventures' December 2025 enterprise survey found 76% of AI use cases were purchased rather than built, up from 53% the year before. But the data also cuts against pure buying. McKinsey's 2026 survey found 32% of organizations decided against purchasing software and built it with coding agents instead, because the economics of building changed. The honest answer is a portfolio.

Buy when

The workflow is undifferentiated (meeting notes, generic support), a category leader exists, and you can accept their data terms. Measure adoption, not licenses. Access does not equal adoption.

Build when

The capability runs on data only you have (underwriting, pricing, operations), it will be sold or will move your valuation, or the SaaS alternative wants your data to train on. FE International's 2026 AI valuation model puts the premium for proprietary AI at 15 to 20%.

Partner when

You need to build but your team ships features, not AI systems. Pick a partner that is model-neutral, transfers knowledge by design, and puts IP ownership in writing. HatchWorks AI's standing position is that clients own what we build for them.

Four questions that settle most build vs buy debates

  1. If this works, is it something a competitor can buy next quarter? If yes, buy it too and compete elsewhere.
  2. Does the value come from the model or from our data and workflow? Model value is rented. Data and workflow value is owned.
  3. Can we swap the underlying model in 12 months without a rebuild? If the answer depends on one vendor, you are buying lock-in.
  4. Who maintains it in year two? If the honest answer is "the consultant," the engagement has no exit.

08 · Data readiness

How do we know if our data is ready for AI?

Short answer

Your data is ready for a specific use case when it is accessible through a governed interface, consistent enough that two systems agree on the same number, and owned by someone accountable for its quality. You do not need a perfect enterprise data platform to start. You need a trustworthy foundation for the first two or three use cases, built so it compounds.

Data readiness is the barrier executives name most often, and the one they most often bring a partner in to solve. The mistake is treating readiness as a prerequisite for the whole enterprise. It is a property of a use case.

A readiness checklist you can run this week

  • Do the systems of record for this use case agree on definitions? (Revenue, customer, location, order)
  • Is there a named data owner per source, with authority to fix quality issues?
  • Can the data be reached through an API, warehouse, or lakehouse, not screenshots and exports?
  • Is there a classification for sensitive fields (PII, PHI, payment data) and a rule for how AI may touch them?
  • Do you know the schema drift history? Legacy ERPs change shape quietly.
  • Is there enough labeled history for the predictions you want? Monthly retraining needs a pipeline, not a project.
  • Is unstructured knowledge (documents, tickets, policies) indexed anywhere, or trapped in inboxes?
  • Can business users validate the output against something they already trust (a dashboard, a report)?

Two patterns from our work. A 52-location franchise system had operations data in one platform and finance in another and no single view. A Databricks lakehouse unified both, a KPI dashboard became the validated source of truth, and a conversational assistant answered 70+ KPI question types with numbers that matched the dashboard, in the CEO's words, "to the penny." A real estate investor needed underwriting predictions inside Salesforce; seven data integrations and Azure medallion pipelines fed three ML models that cut after-repair-value error by 31% and time-to-sell error by 69%. In both cases the data foundation was scoped to the use cases, then extended.

10 · Governance and risk

What does good AI governance look like without slowing everything down?

Short answer

Good governance is woven into the delivery system rather than bolted on at the end: evaluation harnesses that gate every release, human approval points at the decisions that matter, monitoring for drift and misuse, a named owner per system, and an incident process. Reference frameworks are NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001. The test is simple: can you pause any AI system today, and would you know why you should?

The gap is large. IBM's 2026 Tech Leader Study found 77% of technology executives say AI adoption is outpacing governance. SailPoint's 2025 research found 82% of companies use AI agents while only 44% have security policies covering them, and 80% report agents have taken unintended actions. Deloitte found only 21% have a mature governance model for autonomous agents. Stanford's 2026 AI Index counted 362 reported AI incidents in 2025, up 55%.

The governance artifacts a production deployment should produce

  • Use-case register with risk tier, owner, and approval date
  • Evaluation suite with pass thresholds, run on every release (LLM-as-judge plus human sampling)
  • Defined human-in-the-loop points and an off switch per system
  • Monitoring for accuracy, drift, cost per task, and anomalous agent actions
  • Data lineage from source to output, for audit and for debugging
  • Incident runbook: detect, contain, investigate, fix, disclose
  • Model portfolio record: which model, which version, why, and how to swap it
  • Quarterly review with the executive sponsor against the adoption baseline

This is what "Governance woven in over guardrails bolted on" means in the GenDD Manifesto, and it is why our GenEQ deliverables include a Governance Model alongside the adoption plan. Governance that lives in a policy PDF gets ignored. Governance that lives in the pipeline gets enforced.

Agentic AI

What is agentic AI and how is it different from a chatbot?

A chatbot answers. An agent pursues a goal by reasoning over context, deciding on steps, and taking actions in systems through tools, under defined permissions and oversight. The practical difference shows up in architecture: agents need typed tools, permission scopes, evaluation of actions and not only text, and a human decision point you can name.

What is "agent washing" and how do we spot it?

Agent washing is rebranding chatbots, RPA, or simple automations as autonomous agents. Gartner estimates only about 130 of thousands of vendors claiming agentic capability are real. Ask the vendor to show the agent deciding between paths on live data, explain each action it took, and stop mid-task. If it cannot, it is a workflow with a chat window.

Which workflows should go agentic first?

High-volume, clear-outcome, API-reachable workflows with a tolerable error cost: inbound email and document triage, knowledge retrieval and question-answering over trusted data, document generation from structured sources, and steps in the software delivery lifecycle. Avoid starting with irreversible financial or customer-facing actions.

What governance do autonomous agents need?

Scoped permissions per agent and per tool, an action log with lineage, evaluation of actions against expected outcomes, human approval at irreversible steps, cost and rate limits, anomaly monitoring, and a kill switch the business owner can use. Only 44% of organizations using agents have security policies covering them, and 80% report unintended actions. The gap is the risk.

Model selection

Should we standardize on one LLM provider?

No. Standardize on an architecture, not a provider: a routing or abstraction layer that sends each task to the model that fits it on quality, latency, cost, and residency, with evaluations that let you compare models on your data. Most enterprises run two or three models, and routing simple tasks to smaller models saves 40 to 60% of inference cost.

Claude vs OpenAI vs Gemini for enterprise: how do we choose?

Evaluate on your tasks, not benchmarks. Build a small evaluation set from real cases, run each candidate through the same harness, and compare accuracy, latency, cost per task, and behavior on edge cases. Enterprise spend in mid-2025 split roughly 32% Anthropic, 25% OpenAI, 20% Google, which says the market itself is multi-model. Your partner should be certified on more than one.

When do we need open-weight, self-hosted models?

When data residency or regulation prohibits external APIs, when volume is high enough that self-hosting beats per-token pricing, when latency requirements are strict, or when a narrow domain benefits from fine-tuning you want to own outright. Closed models still carry about 87% of enterprise workloads, so treat self-hosting as a targeted decision, not a default.

Can we swap models later without rebuilding?

Yes, if the system was built with a model abstraction layer, prompts and evals stored as versioned assets, and tool interfaces independent of any one provider. Ask to see it. Only 11% of enterprises switched providers in the past year, mostly because their architecture made switching expensive. Build so that switching is a configuration change plus an evaluation run.
